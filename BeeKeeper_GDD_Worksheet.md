## Appendix A. Hive Systems & In-Game Gradation

Hives are the game’s core piece of equipment, and they form a progression ladder in exactly the way bee breeds do — each is a data-defined item with its own stats and trade-offs. The roster below covers the systems Ukrainian beekeepers actually use, plus round, natural and automated designs, sorted into seven tiers from cheap heritage hives up to automated smart apiaries. There is deliberately no single "best" hive: each tier trades yield, winter survival, ease of work, cost and "authenticity" against the others.

The gradation ladder

- Tier 0 — Heritage & Wild: log hives (bort, koloda, carved дуплянка), the straw skep, the clay sapetka and the barrel hive. Cheap or found, atmospheric, barely inspectable — an "authentic/traditional" flavour path.
- Tier 1 — Round, Urban & Bee-centric: the round Sun Hive, the tall urban sculptural tower and the wall-mounted observation hive. A conservation / city style that rewards colony health, pollination and engagement over honey.
- Tier 2 — Beginner Standard: nucleus, Dadant, small lezhak. Where new players start.
- Tier 3 — Traditional Ukrainian: Ukrainian lezhak, 12-frame + magazine, two-body stack. Reliable mid-game hives.
- Tier 4 — Modular / Industrial / Migratory: Langstroth, the Ukrainian Rogati (Palyvoda), and the migratory pavilion. Scaling up and moving to forage.
- Tier 5 — Modern Materials & Natural: insulated foam, Warré, top-bar. Winter-hardy or low-intervention specialists.
- Tier 6 — Automated & Smart: Flow Hive, smart/IoT module, robotic station. End-game labour-savers.

How the gradation works in the game

- Each hive is a ScriptableObject, exactly like your bee breeds: frame system, capacity, cost, yield, winter survival, management ease, plus special flags (auto-harvest, sensor-monitored, non-inspectable, migratory). New hives are data, not code.
- Unlocks are gated by money + milestones + skill: starter and beginner tiers are available from the start; industrial, modern and automated tiers open as the player hits honey / hive-count / expansion milestones and can afford them.
- Trade-offs drive the choice — there is no single "best" hive: heritage is cheap and atmospheric but tiny-yield with no disease control; horizontal lezhaks are easy and back-friendly; Langstroth/modular maxes yield but demands lifting and skill; foam wins winter; Flow skips extraction; the smart module skips inspection.
- Synergy with bee breeds: e.g. Carpathian or Buckfast in a Rogati/Langstroth for migratory honey runs; Carniolan in an insulated foam hive for winter survival; a gentle Italian colony in a Flow or Sun hive for low-disturbance harvesting.
- On-foot mode hooks: framed hives feed the pull-a-frame / diagnose loop; skep, bort and koloda cannot be opened conventionally — a deliberate limitation that defines the heritage playstyle; a Flow hive replaces the extractor minigame with a tap-the-spout interaction; a smart hive surfaces problems as alerts in the overview HUD instead of a manual inspection.
- Placement & regulation layer (QUESTIONS.md #23): non-inspectable heritage hives could be restricted near the school/playground, while smart, monitored hives could satisfy those rules — a light educational/challenge system.
- City vs. countryside forms: the urban sculptural tower and the wall-mounted observation hive are the "city apiary" answer — compact, glass-fronted and approval-friendly for placement among the recreated Sknyliv blocks — while the carved дуплянка, sapetka and barrel hive lean the other way as decorative heritage pieces that raise the apiary’s prestige/atmosphere score more than its honey.
## Appendix B. Bee Breeds & In-Game Gradation

Bees are the game’s other core data-defined item, and like the hives in Appendix A they form a trade-off ladder rather than a straight ranking — each breed is a ScriptableObject with its own stats and drawbacks, and there is deliberately no single “best” bee. The roster spans gentle, high-output starter bees; hardy Ukrainian and European locals built for the winter; mite-resistant and hard-mode specialists; one exotic managed species; and a set of wild solitary bees that make no honey but pollinate. Where a stat is a number it is seeded from real subspecies data (the Eversweet Apiaries breed-trait chart in the reference folder) and rounded to the game’s 1–5 scale, so the balancing starts from how these bees actually behave.

How breeds differ mechanically

A breed is not a re-skin. Every colony runs on the same handful of levers, and the breed decides where each one sits — how fast the family grows, how readily it makes new queens and swarms, how angry it gets when opened, how much honey it packs away, how it survives winter, and how well it shrugs off mites and disease. Four of those levers are the mechanics you asked to lead with; the rest are the supporting trade-offs that keep the choice interesting.

The four core mechanics

- Colony growth / buildup — how fast the family gets bigger. It governs how quickly a small package or nucleus becomes a full-strength foraging colony each spring. A fast builder (Carniolan, Russian, African) fills its boxes early and out-forages a slow starter (Caucasian, European Dark), but is far likelier to run out of room and swarm. Growth feeds population, and population feeds honey yield.
- Breeding & reproduction — how the colony makes new queens and multiplies. Two linked systems: swarming (an overcrowded colony raises a new queen and half the bees leave — a free new colony if you catch and re-hive them, lost bees if you don’t) and cross-breeding (put a queen of one breed over another breed’s drones to blend their stats and roll for a hybrid trait). Buckfast is the in-fiction proof this works. Swarm-happy breeds propagate cheaply but need watching; low-swarm breeds (Buckfast, Caucasian, Carpathian) are stable but slower to multiply.
- Aggressiveness / temperament — how defensive the bees are when you open the hive. It is shown as Calm (5 = docile); aggressiveness is simply its inverse (6 − Calm). It drives sting risk in on-foot mode, how much smoke a hive needs before you can work it, and whether the hive is even legal to place near the school or playground (QUESTIONS.md #23). A gentle Buckfast or Caucasian can be worked in a T-shirt; an African colony is hard-mode and would fail placement rules.
- Everything else the breed dials — Honey yield (nectar-to-honey output), Winter hardiness (survival through a Lviv winter), and Mite/disease resistance (varroa, tracheal mite and nosema — how often you must inspect and treat), plus supporting traits from the reference chart: foraging earliness and range, propolis output (the Caucasian specialty) and pollen output (the Carniolan strength). These are the trade-offs that stop any single breed from being strictly best.

The caste model (worker / queen / drone)

Every colony is one queen, a population of workers and a seasonal cohort of drones — the three body types in the reference size chart, where the worker is smallest, the drone is bulkier and the queen is the long-bodied one. In sim terms the queen is a single unit whose quality sets buildup speed and breeding (lay rate and genetics); the workers are the population number that produces honey and forages; and drones appear only in the warm season to mate — they carry the breed’s genetics for cross-breeding and do no work. Requeening (swapping the queen) is how the player changes a colony’s breed and resets its stats, and losing the queen with no replacement collapses the colony — the mechanic already stubbed as QueenStatus in HiveController.

A dash means the stat does not apply — solitary bees keep no colony and store no honey; their value is pollination, noted in the role column. Numbers are tunable balancing placeholders seeded from real subspecies traits.

How the gradation works in the game

- Each breed is a ScriptableObject — exactly like your hives: a name, the six rated stats above, a foraging range, propolis / pollen output and flags (solitary / pollinator-only, cold-sensitive, aggressive). New breeds are data, not code — extend BeeBreedData with growthRate, swarmTendency, aggressiveness, winterHardiness and mite-resistance fields alongside the honeyYield, temperament and coldTolerance it already has.
- Unlocks gated by money + milestones + region — the gentle local bees (Carpathian, Ukrainian Steppe, Carniolan) are the sensible Lviv starters; Buckfast and Italian come cheap early; the mite-tank Russian, the exotic Asian bee and the hard-mode African open later behind honey / expansion milestones and cost real money.
- Trade-offs drive the choice — no single best breed: Italian and Carniolan max yield but eat their stores and swarm; Carpathian and Ukrainian Steppe win the winter and stay calm; Caucasian gives the best propolis but starts slow and dislikes cold; Russian and African shrug off varroa but bring swarming and (African) real danger; European Dark is a frugal survivor that is slow and defensive.
- Breeding & cross-breeding — swarm-prone breeds hand you free splits if you catch them; requeen a colony with a bought or reared queen to change its breed; put a queen over another breed’s drones to blend stats and roll for a hybrid — the systemic version of how Buckfast was made.
- Synergy with hives (Appendix A) — Carniolan or Carpathian in an insulated foam hive for winter survival; Carpathian or Buckfast in a Rogati / Langstroth for migratory honey runs; a gentle Italian or Caucasian in a Flow or Sun hive for low-disturbance harvesting; the wild solitary bees pair with the urban / observation and heritage forms as conservation pieces.
- On-foot hooks — aggressive breeds demand more smoke and carry real sting risk if you skip the smoker (QUESTIONS.md #10); calm breeds are forgiving. Solitary-bee “hotels” are never opened or inspected — they just raise the area’s pollination score.
- Placement & regulation (QUESTIONS.md #23) — calm, low-swarm breeds and non-stinging solitary bees satisfy the rules near the school and playground; the African colony (and swarmy heritage setups) would be restricted there.
- Setting fit — Carpathian (Karpatka) and the Ukrainian Steppe bee are the local, historically-correct bees for a Lviv apiary and make the natural default roster; Italian, Carniolan and Buckfast are the familiar imports; African and the Asian honey bee are deliberate exotics for later-game variety.

Art & model notes

The breeds read differently enough to tell apart at a glance — the reference photos in the folder back this up, and it is worth baking into the bee models and hive-preview art:

- Italian / Cordovan — the lightest bees: golden-amber with clear leather-yellow bands. Cordovan is a reddish colour morph of the same bee (cosmetic — a prestige unlock, not a stat change).
- Carniolan / Carpathian — dark grey-brown bodies with pale grey hair bands — the classic “grey bee” look; Carpathian reads a touch greyer as the local mountain strain.
- Caucasian — dark with grey pubescence and a visibly long tongue (proboscis) in inspection close-ups — sell it as the propolis specialist.
- Ukrainian Steppe — grey with warm yellowish tones, slightly smaller and neat; the reference family shot shows the tight, calm cluster on the comb.
- European Dark, Russian & African — darker, stockier and near-black; animate the Russian and African more nervous and defensive to sell their temperament versus the placid grey bees.
- Wild solitary bees — deliberately off-model from the honeybees: the Carpenter bee is big, shiny and almost black; Mason and Hairy-Footed Flower bees are rounder and fuzzier. Use them as bee-hotel props, not hive occupants. The caste size chart (worker < drone < queen, with the long-bodied queen) sets the relative scale for the worker / queen / drone models inside every managed colony.

## Appendix D. Design Review — An Outside Read on This Draft

This appendix and the two that follow were added on top of the worksheet without altering anything written above them: no decision, draft answer or roster entry in Sections 1–7 or Appendices A–C has been changed or removed. What follows is commentary and options, not decisions — where the document had already settled something, this read treats it as settled and flags at most the tradeoff that came with it.

What is working

- The hive ladder (Tier 0–6) and breed ladder (Tier A–E) are real trade-off matrices, not power curves. Langstroth maxes yield but demands lifting; foam wins winter but costs "authentic" look; Flow skips extraction but needs warm weather; Carniolan explosive-builds but swarms; Caucasian is gentlest but slow and cold-averse. Nothing dominates. This is the strongest piece of design in the doc — it makes "which hive/breed" a real decision, not a checklist, and it will still be interesting at hour 40.
- The setting hands you content for free. A real place with real subspecies means seasons, predators (varroa, hornets), forage geography, and even regulation (hives near the school/kindergarten/playground) all arrive pre-motivated by biology and geography rather than invented from scratch — a "world bible" few solo devs get without writing it themselves.
- The core loop is already narratable end to end. Section 4's decided on-foot loop ("wake up > check hives > work the apiary > workbench > shop/catch rogue families > return home > end day") is concrete enough to build from. Most GDDs at this stage never get past vague verbs; this one already has a walkable day.
- Data-driven architecture matches the design's own philosophy. BeeBreedData and the implied hive ScriptableObjects mean the roster can grow to the full 15 breeds / 25 hive types as .asset files, not new classes — the doc's "new breeds are data, not code" line is honored by the actual codebase, a rare consistency for a project where programmer time is the scarcest resource.
- Milestone + soft-win + sandbox is the right shape for a hobby project. It doesn't need a hard ending to feel finished, and the milestone list (honey totals, surviving a winter, unlocking breeds, full expansion) reads as achievable in stages, not one distant finish line.
- Scope discipline has already been exercised once. Genericizing real brand names and hand-building geometry instead of importing OSM/3D Tiles both traded fidelity for a smaller, controllable build — proof the project can make that trade when it needs to.

The three biggest risks

1. Scope has been accumulating faster than it's been cut. Named in the worksheet: 25 hive types across 7 tiers, 15 bee breeds across 5 tiers, plus vertical farming, a car and bicycle, a post office/Nova Poshta delivery loop, online ordering, shops, a mansion rebuild, a books-as-research tree, an automation/computer layer, wild-swarm hunting and taming, requeening, splitting, and cross-breeding — on top of a colony simulation and 2+ km of hand-built city. Any one of these is a plausible solo-dev project on its own. Section 6's own tip says to "ruthlessly cut your first version to the core sim loop + one bee breed + basic economy" — right, but nothing else enforces it; every other section keeps adding surface area with no stated cut-line for v1 versus aspirational.

2. The committed MVP doesn't test the fun the design claims to sell. PLAN.md/CLAUDE.md commit to "sim core: one hive, one breed, basic economy" as first playable, and the code (HiveController accumulating honey from breed × population × health, EconomyManager.SellHoney/ TrySpend) is exactly that — a numbers loop with no player action beyond placing a hive and clicking sell. But the draft pitch says the core feeling is "the calm... meditatory experience of apiary," and perspective is locked as "1st person 80% of the time," with the fun explicitly in hands-on hive work (smoke, pull frames, diagnose, harvest). The MVP as scoped proves whether a spreadsheet is satisfying to watch tick up, not whether the on-foot ritual is fun. Section 6's own "prove it's fun first" tip is being pointed at the wrong candidate.

3. The perspective decision stranded the top-down work the rest of the doc still assumes. Section 2's draft answer locks top-down as a late-game unlock ("when the apiary/farm is big enough he can buy computer"), not a mode the player lives in early. But PLAN.md's original "Gameplay loop: Hybrid" entry, the whole CityOverview/ orthographic-camera architecture, and the just-built plans/hive-placement.md are all top-down-first work built before automation is purchasable. Two costs follow: already-built/planned work belongs to a mode that (per the newer decision) shouldn't exist until late-game, and the semi-realistic Cities:Skylines-register city art was styled for a top-down camera but will mostly be seen at eye level, where extruded block-out footprints won't hold up the way they would from above.

Tensions to resolve

- Calm core vs. punishing fail state. "The core feeling... is calm and healthy meditatory experience" (Section 1) sits against "all money and bee families lost," with "a health number that will be decline on bee/wasp/hornet stings" (Section 4). Not automatically incompatible, but nothing explains how harshly that fail state bites or how a calm loop survives a real threat of total loss.
- Two perspective decisions are both still "current." Section 2's question block frames the loop as overview-first: "overview -> place/ inspect hives -> manage foraging & economy -> drop on-foot." Section 2's draft-answer block frames it as on-foot-first: "1st person 80% of the time... buy computer and start topdown... for automation." Both are written as settled in the same section, describing materially different games.
- District-scale escalation vs. late-unlocked top-down. Section 3 maps escalation like Diablo's dungeon — "one backyard hive -> neighborhood apiary -> the whole district" — and PLAN.md treats seeing/expanding across the district as mid-game. If top-down is genuinely gated behind buying a computer post-automation, the player may reach "whole district" scale while only ever seeing it on foot or through a laptop.
- An errand-running loop under a meditative pitch. The decided on-foot loop includes shopping, buying catching materials, and searching woods/mountains/fields for wild bees, plus (still open) driving, a post office, and online ordering — a lot of commuting under a game whose stated core feeling is calm. Worth naming whether travel is meditative pacing or friction between the meditative parts.
- The MVP has no on-foot component, but on-foot is where queen/health mechanics are meant to be discovered. Appendix B notes requeening is "already stubbed as QueenStatus," and the on-foot loop is where the player would act on it. The current MVP changes that stat in the background with no player-facing moment attached — the mechanic exists in code before it exists in play.

Where the design is thin

- Economy: sources, almost no sinks or numbers. QUESTIONS.md #14/#15 are open; the only numbers anywhere (goldBalance = 100, honeyPricePerKg = 15) are an illustrative placeholder in plans/hive-placement.md, not a locked decision. No stated harvest yield in kg, no ongoing costs (fuel, frames, treatment, permits) to spend gold on — sources with no sinks don't produce decisions, just a number that goes up.
- Time and season pacing exist only as adjectives. "A day passes in minutes, a season over several in-game days" is a direction, not a number — nothing states the real-second length of a day/season or the tick interval the (unbuilt) central hive ticker should run on, and that number paces every other rate in the sim.
- Minute-to-minute first person is undefined. The on-foot loop is specified at day scale ("check the bee hives... work around the bee hives..."), with no description of what a single hive interaction feels like as input/feedback — length, clicks, what a diagnosis shows. For the system carrying 80% of play time, this is the least-detailed part of the document.
- Automation is undefined despite being structurally load-bearing. The whole perspective model hinges on "buy computer -> automation -> top-down unlocks," but nothing states what automation changes mechanically — auto-harvest, auto-treat, or just alerts (per Appendix A's "smart hive... cuts manual inspection"). The one system the camera structure pivots on currently has zero mechanical definition.

One recommendation

Redefine the first playable slice around the on-foot moment, not the top-down numbers loop: one hive, one breed, and a walkable day-cycle — wake, walk to the hive, smoke it, pull a frame, see a diagnosis, harvest, sell, sleep — as the thing that has to prove itself fun before city geometry, before the breed/hive ladders expand, and before the top-down CityOverview/placement work goes further. The doc already agrees the fun is the meditative experience delivered through "1st person 80% of the time" hands-on work, not honeyStoredKg climbing — so the "prove it's fun first" checkpoint should point at the mechanic the pitch is actually betting on. This doesn't discard the sim-core code (HiveController/EconomyManager still back the simulation underneath); it changes what "first playable" means from an accumulation-and-sell loop to the smallest slice of the hands-on ritual — cheaper to build (one hive, one small room, no city block-out) and a more honest fun-test.

## Appendix E. Mechanics Roster — Systems Derivable From This Document

Every mechanic below is derived from something already stated in BeeKeeper_GDD_Worksheet.docx, PLAN.md, or the two appendices — this is a systematisation of the existing draft, not a wishlist. Where an existing data ladder (hive Yield/Winter/Mgmt, breed Yield/Grow/Calm/Wint/Rsist/Swrm, the special flags: auto-harvest, sensor-monitored, non-inspectable, migratory) already covers a mechanic, it's reused rather than re-invented; where a mechanic needs a field that doesn't exist yet, that's called out.

Build tier: Core is capped near 10 entries and must together form one complete, playable loop — this is a strict reading of "prove the sim core is fun before adding more" (Section 6's tip).

Colony & Hive Simulation

- Colony population — the hive holds a population number that grows or shrinks over time; it's the multiplier honey yield is built on. Source: CLAUDE.md ("HiveController accumulates honey from breed × population × health"). Depends on: none. Build tier: Core
- Colony health — a meter representing overall hive condition, feeding into the honey formula and standing in for the not-yet-built pest/disease system until it exists. Source: CLAUDE.md, PLAN.md ("Hive placement & management (capacity, health, queen status)"). Depends on: none. Build tier: Core
- Honey accumulation — honey stored (honeyStoredKg) rises over time as a function of breed yield × population × health, scaled by in-game time rather than wall-clock time. Source: CLAUDE.md, PLAN.md. Depends on: colony population, colony health, breed data. Build tier: Core
- Central hive ticker — a single fixed-interval in-game-time tick that drives every hive's simulation step, replacing per-frame Update() work so cost doesn't scale with hive count. Source: CLAUDE.md's explicit warning + plans/hive-placement.md's placed-hives list as the ticker's future hook. Depends on: none. Build tier: Core
- Queen status — present/absent (and eventually quality) state that, when lost with no replacement, collapses the colony. Source: Appendix B ("losing the queen with no replacement collapses the colony — the mechanic already stubbed as QueenStatus in HiveController"). Depends on: colony population/health. Build tier: Core
- Overcrowding / capacity trigger — each hive's frame count/capacity (already in the Appendix A roster) caps population growth and, once exceeded, is the precondition for swarming. Source: Appendix A "Frames / cap." column. Depends on: colony population, hive data. Build tier: Second pass

Queens, Breeding & Genetics

- Requeening — the player replaces a hive's queen (bought or reared), changing the colony's effective breed and resetting its stats. Source: Appendix B ("Requeening... is how the player changes a colony's breed and resets its stats"). Depends on: queen status, breed data. Build tier: Second pass
- Swarming — an overcrowded/neglected colony raises a new queen and half the population leaves as a wild swarm; unattended, that population is simply lost. Source: Appendix B "Breeding & reproduction" and QUESTIONS.md #5. Depends on: overcrowding trigger, breed Swrm stat. Build tier: Second pass
- Catching a runaway swarm — the player locates and re-hives a swarm before it's lost for good, turning an in-progress loss into a free new colony. Source: Appendix B ("a free new colony if you catch and re-hive them, lost bees if you don't"); on-foot loop's "catch the rogue families." Depends on: swarming. Build tier: Second pass
- Splitting a colony — the player deliberately divides an overcrowded colony into a second hive rather than waiting for a swarm to force it. Source: on-foot loop ("decide if they are good for division"). Depends on: overcrowding trigger, an owned empty hive box. Build tier: Second pass
- Cross-breeding — placing a queen of one breed over another breed's drones blends their stats and rolls for a hybrid trait; Buckfast is the in-fiction proof it works. Source: Appendix B "Breeding & reproduction," "the systemic version of how Buckfast was made." Depends on: requeening, breed data ladder. New field needed: a hybrid-trait roll table. Build tier: Later

Harvest & Products

- Harvest action — the player converts accumulated stored honey into a sellable batch; this is the discrete action the on-foot loop names ("collect frames with the honey") sitting on top of continuous accumulation. Source: on-foot loop; CLAUDE.md's honeyStoredKg. Depends on: honey accumulation. Build tier: Core
- Honey extractor — a workbench tool that spins honey out of pulled frames, the standard-tier harvesting minigame/interaction. Source: on-foot loop ("use honey extractor with different frames"). Depends on: harvest action, workbench. Build tier: Second pass
- Flow-tap harvesting — Tier 6 hives with the auto-harvest flag skip the extractor entirely; the player taps a spout instead. Source: Appendix A Tier 6 roster + "a Flow hive replaces the extractor minigame with a tap-the-spout interaction." Depends on: harvest action, a Tier 6 hive owned. Build tier: Later
- Wax product chain — wax collected alongside honey feeds a separate sale/crafting chain (e.g. candles). Source: QUESTIONS.md #3; Appendix B role notes (top-bar hives: "more wax, less honey"). New field needed: wax output per hive/breed. Depends on: harvest action. Build tier: Second pass
- Propolis product chain — propolis output is a named specialty of the Caucasian breed and feeds its own sale chain. Source: Appendix B ("the propolis champion (feeds a wax / propolis sale chain)"). New field needed: propolis output per breed. Depends on: harvest action. Build tier: Second pass
- Pollen product chain — pollen output is a named Carniolan strength and likewise a separate saleable product. Source: Appendix B ("pollen output (the Carniolan strength)"). New field needed: pollen output per breed. Depends on: harvest action. Build tier: Second pass

Hive Crafting, Repair & Equipment

- Hive placement — the player places a purchased hive on valid ground, cost-gated through the economy. Source: plans/hive-placement.md (already substantially built). Depends on: economy: spend gold. Build tier: Core
- Workbench: build a hive — the supplementary building near the mansion holds a workbench where new hives are constructed from materials. Source: PLAN.md ("a workbench for creating hives and repairing them"); on-foot loop ("build new hives, repair them, build new frames"). Depends on: hive placement. Build tier: Second pass
- Workbench: repair a hive — damaged/worn hives are restored at the workbench. Source: same as above. New field needed: a hive durability/wear stat, not currently part of the Appendix A roster. Depends on: workbench build. Build tier: Second pass

## Appendix G. Resolved Answers & Additional Design Ideas

This appendix converts the highest-value open questions - the “Questions for you” blocks in sections 1–7, QUESTIONS.md #1–#40, and Appendix F #41–#95 - into concrete, opinionated decisions, and adds design ideas the worksheet is currently missing. It does not alter sections 1–7 or appendixes A–F; it stands on top of them. Every answer is meant to be consistent with the locked PLAN.md decisions and with the hive/breed rosters in Appendixes A and B. Question numbers are cross-referenced in parentheses so each answer traces back to its source. Any hard number here is a first-pass value labelled “(placeholder for the sim-balance pass)” - it fixes an order of magnitude and a direction, not a final constant.

Vision & the one-paragraph pitch

- The pitch (§1, #24, #25). “Bee Keeper Simulator is a cozy management-and-inspection sim about reviving your grandfather’s apiary in a hand-recreated slice of Sknyliv, Lviv - you place and tend real bee subspecies across a living neighbourhood, running the whole operation top-down from a computer or walking up to any hive to smoke it, pull a frame, and read the colony with your own eyes.” It is for cozy-sim and management players, beekeeping enthusiasts, and people with a connection to Lviv - built private-first, but written so it could face outward later without a redesign.
- Core feeling: mastery of a living system (§1, #23). Of the three candidate feelings (calm / mastery / pride-of-rebuilding) the game sells mastery - the quiet competence of reading a hive and being right. Calm is the surface texture and rebuilding the mansion is the long-arc reward, but the moment-to-moment hook is “I understood what this colony needed before it failed.” This choice drives the whole design: it is why diagnosis is skill-based (#71), why failure is recoverable rather than punitive (#85), and why breeds are trade-offs, not tiers.
- The single distinctive thing (§1 vision block). Pick ONE for marketing and for internal focus: the real bee-subspecies behaviour model. The real-place recreation is expensive and can be quietly downscaled (#40); the sim + on-foot hybrid is a structure, not a hook. But “each real subspecies actually plays differently” is the line a reviewer repeats, and it is the cheapest of the three to make excellent because it is pure data (Appendix B).
- Platform & release lane (#26, #72–#75). Build for this machine on Windows/Linux desktop, keyboard-and-mouse first. Name the eventual lane without committing: a premium one-off on itch.io / Steam next to APICO, Hokko Life, and Farming Simulator. No storefront work happens until the sim core is proven fun (#39).

The core loop, narrated as one concrete cycle

- One 60-second cycle (§2 core-loop, §4 one-cycle). From the top-down computer view the player scans the district, spots a hive badge flipped to “ready” (honey full) and one flashing amber (swarm-risk). They click the full hive to sell its batch through the economy panel, then drop to on-foot at the amber hive: walk up, light the smoker, lift the lid, pull two frames, read overcrowding on the comb, and add a super (or split the colony) to defuse the swarm. Back to top-down, advance time, watch foragers stream toward the park bloom, and repeat. Place → tend → harvest → diagnose → sell → expand is the spine.
- First five minutes (§4 first-5-min, #59). Launch drops the player at the inherited apiary with the grandfather present as a one-scene guide (#63). He walks them through placing one beginner Dadant (Tier 2) hive, installing a starter Carpathian nucleus (the local, gentle bee - Appendix B), one on-foot inspection, and one honey sale. Onboarding is a guided first hive, not a separate tutorial mode; it stays replayable as a “how do I…” reference from the book/help menu (#65).
- Session length & pull-back (#65-session). A satisfying sitting is 20–40 minutes - roughly one to two in-game days. The pull-back is anticipation state: a colony mid-buildup, a super that will be full by tomorrow, a wild swarm reported in the woods, or a market day on the calendar. Always leave one clock running that the player wants to come back and check.
- Time-decoupling for possible co-op (§2 multiplayer). Confirmed single-player. The central hive ticker consumes an in-game-time delta and a world-state object only - never reads Input directly - so simulation stays authority-clean if co-op is ever wanted, without paying any networking cost now.

Signature system - the one system reviewers name

- The “DRLG” is the living colony sim driven by real subspecies data (§2 signature, §6 hardest-problem, #36). Formally: a central ticker advances every hive on in-game time, and each colony’s behaviour - buildup speed, swarm pressure, temperament, winter survival, yield - falls out of its BeeBreedData stats crossed with its hive’s ScriptableObject and the local forage/weather. It is the signature because it is where the real bees become gameplay, it feeds both modes (top-down badges and on-foot frames read the same state), and it is the thing the whole document already leans on.

Concrete milestone & career goals

- Milestone ladder (§2 progression, #20). Five staged goals, each unlocking a district band and a hive/breed tier, then open sandbox after. M1 - Rooted: 3 hives, survive your first winter with all colonies alive, 20 kg honey sold. M2 - Established: 8 hives, 3 breeds in use, 100 kg cumulative, unlock Tier 3–4 hives. M3 - Apiarist: 15 hives across two districts, first successful requeen and first caught swarm, 400 kg cumulative. M4 - District Keeper: expand across the full recreated polygon, 8+ breeds unlocked, a named honey varietal certified (see Appendix H). M5 - The Homestead: rebuild the solarpunk mansion and reach the soft win. All kg/hive numbers are placeholders for the sim-balance pass.
- Unlocking all 15 breeds is a completionist chase, not required (#58). A normal M1–M5 run reaches 8–10 breeds; the last few (exotic/hybrid) are optional sandbox goals so the soft win never depends on grinding every subspecies.
- Expansion is gated by money + milestone, not free (§2 scope, #19, #76). Each new district band unlocks at its milestone AND costs a site/land fee, so growth is a deliberate spend, not an automatic reveal. The apiary-candidate zone in the map JSON (the forest/waterfall side) is the confirmed early expansion target for M2–M3.

Colony-sim depth

- Aggregate stats, not individual bees (#2, #52). A colony is a small struct - population (one integer), plus three bucketed role fractions (nurse/forager/guard) derived from it, not per-bee agents. Individual bees exist only as visual flourish (foragers streaming to bloom); they are never simulated entities. This keeps a district of dozens of hives inside the central-ticker budget.
- Hive health is several meters, surfaced as one (#49). Under the hood: food stores, mite/disease load, and queen status are separate values; the top-down badge shows a single 0–100 health roll-up, but on-foot inspection exposes the underlying meters. This is what makes diagnosis a skill - the summary hides the cause, the frame reveals it.
- Zero health is a recoverable “critical” state, not instant death (#51, #85). A collapsing colony enters a visible critical window (days, breed-dependent) where feeding, requeening, or treatment can still save it. Only an ignored critical colony is permanently lost - and even a total wipe drops the player to a recoverable rock-bottom (one hive, a small stake), never a hard game over. Cozy sim, not permadeath (§4 fail-state).
- Queen status is a small object, not a bool (#4, #50, #53). It tracks present/absent plus a 1–5 quality that decays with age and lifts on requeen. Requeening is an active player action with two routes: buy a queen of a chosen breed from the shop (fast, costs gold) or rear one from the colony over in-game days (free, slower, needs a healthy colony). Losing the queen with no replacement is the classic failure spiral.
- Swarming & splitting are the same mechanic, one accidental and one deliberate (#5, #55–#57). A colony past a breed-scaled crowding threshold (its Swrm stat, Appendix B) flips to swarm-risk. Ignore it and half the colony leaves as a catchable wild swarm in the woods - a loss you can partly recover by finding and re-hiving it (#56). Pre-empt it by deliberately splitting into an owned empty hive box: costs the box + a little time, yields a free new colony (#57). Swarm-prone breeds are a double-edged gift.
- Pest/disease is a small set of named threats, not abstract sliders (#6, #49). Ship four: varroa mites, hive beetles, foulbrood, wax moths, each with a distinct on-frame visual tell and a matching treatment/tool. Under the hood they still feed the disease-load meter, but the player diagnoses a named thing, which is what makes inspection feel like real beekeeping rather than watching a bar.
- Weather bites day-to-day, seasons set the envelope (#7, #80–#82). Rain and cold snaps suppress foraging that day; heatwaves stress water needs; season sets the bloom curve and the winter survival window. Winter is a gradual, visible drain the player can intervene in (feed, insulate), not a single pass/fail roll at winter’s end (#82) - the drama is watching stores fall and deciding when to act.
- Foraging is abstracted with a light visual layer (#8, #79). The number that matters is per-zone forage density feeding each colony’s range-and-yield formula; the streaming forager particles are cosmetic and read-only. No real pathfinding - the district can hold many hives without a flight-sim budget.

Economy

- Currency: hryvnia (₴), not abstract coin (#14, #41). The setting is a real Lviv neighbourhood; grounded local money reinforces that and costs nothing extra. EconomyManager’s internal field can stay generically named, but every UI-facing label reads ₴/hryvnia. (Trade-off: ties prices to a real currency the player may have intuitions about - fine for a grounded sim, and it is exactly the kind of authenticity the real-place premise is selling.)
- Products sell on their own prices, not one blended value (#3, #46). Honey, wax, propolis, and pollen each have a distinct price and feed the chains already named in Appendix E - wax→candles, honey→mead, propolis and pollen as breed-specialty products. Distinct prices are what make breed choice matter (a Caucasian’s propolis, a Carniolan’s pollen) and give the market variety without new systems.
- Price model: quality- and varietal-driven, with mild market drift (#15). Base price per product, multiplied by a quality/purity factor the player cultivates, plus small seasonal demand drift and a spike on market/festival days. No punishing market crashes - the interesting lever is “better honey is worth more,” which rewards good keeping.
- Contracts exist alongside instant sell (#16). Instant sell-for-gold is always available as the baseline; on top, recurring customers post contracts (“5 kg linden honey to the market by Friday”) that pay a premium and build a standing relationship. Contracts are the pull-back hook and the reason to specialise a varietal, not a mandatory chore.
- Money sinks (#45). Hive purchase per tier, land/site fees per district expansion, tool + smoker-fuel + treatment consumables, books/research, workbench repairs on worn hives, and the big long-arc sink - rebuilding the mansion. Enough sinks that gold never just piles up uselessly.
- First-pass numbers (placeholders for the sim-balance pass). Starting balance ₴1,000 (up from the plans/hive-placement.md stub of 100, which is too thin to place a real first hive); honey base price ₴180/kg; a healthy beginner Dadant colony yields on the order of 10–20 kg across a good season (#42, #43). A “harvest” is a discrete action converting a hive’s stored honey into a sellable batch (#44). These fix scale and direction only - final tuning is the sim-balance agent’s job.

On-foot moment-to-moment

- Toolkit is bought, upgraded, and consumed (#9, #63-tool). Smoker (burns fuel), hive tool, bee brush, and protective suit (wears down, better tiers cut sting risk). Consumables (smoker fuel, treatments) are the recurring small spend; tools/suit are tiered upgrades that measurably change inspection speed and safety, not cosmetics (#13).
- Sting risk is real and consequence-bearing but never lethal (#10, #83–#85). Skipping the smoker or handling an aggressive breed (Appendix B temperament) risks stings that drain a dedicated player-health meter. At zero: a temporary incapacitation - can’t work that in-game day - not a game over. Aggressive breeds and heritage non-openable hives shape how carefully you work.
- Diagnosis is visual pattern-spotting, scaling by hive tier (#11, #71). On framed hives the player pulls the frame and reads the comb model for the named tells (mites, beetles, foulbrood, moths, overcrowding). Higher-tier smart hives (Tier 6, Appendix A) hand you a sensor readout instead - automation you pay for. This makes diagnosis skill the core competence early and a convenience you can buy your way past late.
- Frame-level detail, kept fast (#12, #69–#70). Opening a framed hive is a short sequence (approach → smoke → lift lid → pull frame), and the player pulls individual frames rather than getting one aggregate reveal - that is where the mastery lives. One inspection is a repeatable 15–30-second action so a full apiary round stays pleasant, not a slog.
- Harvest logistics: carry frames to the extractor, with an automation escape hatch (#72). By default harvesting means pulling honey frames and carrying them to the workbench/extractor - a light inventory step that gives the on-foot mode weight. Tier 6 flow-tap hives (Appendix A) and the in-game computer skip the carry, which is exactly what the player is buying when they automate (#73).

World & city

- NPCs: ambient, lightweight (#21). Sparse pedestrians and occasional traffic for life, plus a few named, interactable NPCs (the market vendor, the bee-supply shopkeeper, the grandfather). Not a simulated citizenry - atmosphere and a couple of relationship anchors, nothing that competes with the hive-count budget.
- One rival beekeeper, as flavour and light pressure (#22). A single AI apiary exists in the district - it does not truly compete for forage math (that would be opaque and unfun), but it posts rival contract bids and reacts to your reputation, giving the world a peer without a hidden economy war.
- Placement regulation near school/playground is a real rule, softly taught (#23). Aggressive breeds and non-inspectable heritage hives are restricted within a radius of the mapped school #40, kindergarten #75, and playground; calm/low-swarm breeds and closed observation hives are allowed. It is a light educational-and-challenge layer, surfaced as a placement warning, not a fine system.
- Getting around: walk early, bike/car unlock later, fast-travel to known sites (#25, #88). Modes differ mechanically - walking is free/slow; a bike is faster; a car carries more harvest and reaches the far district band. Once a hive site is established, fast-travel between saved sites so traversal never becomes the game. This ties into the Nova Poshta ordering loop (#89) as the no-travel way to restock consumables for a shipping fee.
- Map boundary: the red polygon is the project boundary; the background ridge is skybox (#26, #76–#78). Expansion happens inside the polygon across milestone bands; the mountain ridge stays a non-walkable backdrop. This keeps the hand-built city scope bounded and honest - the escape-hatch of a simpler fictional town (#40) stays available only if the core district itself proves too heavy.

Player character & narrative

- Light customization, fixed-enough protagonist (#27). Choose name, a few appearance/suit options, and gender presentation; the character is otherwise fixed so the grandfather/inheritance framing stays coherent. Cheap to build, and it lets the player see themselves in the apiary without a full character creator.
- A small player-skill layer, separate from hive/breed upgrades (#28). A few earned proficiencies - faster inspections, calmer handling (less sting risk), better diagnosis accuracy - grown by doing, not a big tree. It rewards playtime and makes the on-foot loop feel like the player personally getting better, which is on-theme with the mastery core feeling.
- Narrative framing: inherit the grandfather’s apiary and rebuild toward the solarpunk homestead (#29, #63–#65). Locked to match Appendix E/F: the grandfather runs the tutorial in person, then recurs as an occasional letter/quest-giver (his advice unlocks small things), and the long arc is restoring the neglected apiary and mansion. His fate is left warm-ambiguous - present in memory and letters - rather than a grief beat; this is a cozy game (#64).

Presentation & tech

- HUD: hybrid, leaning diegetic (#32). On-foot is mostly diegetic - you read the hive, not a panel. Top-down is an honest sim HUD (hive badges, economy panel, calendar). The in-game computer literally IS the top-down HUD in fiction (#74), which resolves the diegetic-vs-menu tension neatly: the sim UI is the machine you bought.
- Audio: ambient countryside, hive-forward (#31). A calm, largely ambient bed - wind, birds, the varying hum of a healthy vs. queenless hive as an audible diagnostic cue - with light, warm melodic themes for market days and milestones. The hive’s sound doing real diagnostic work is a cheap, distinctive touch.
- Accessibility from the start (#33). Colorblind-safe hive badges (shape + colour, never colour alone, which matters because health/alerts are colour-coded), fully remappable controls, and text-size/subtitle options. Cheapest when designed in now rather than retrofitted.
- Controls: keyboard + mouse first, gamepad-friendly design (#34). Ship KB+M; keep interactions to a small verb set and radial-friendly context actions so a gamepad layer is a later addition, not a redesign.
- Save system: single continuous save + autosave, no scumming the fail state (#36, #93). Because failure is recoverable-not-fatal (#85), the save system does not need to protect against a harsh wipe; a single continuous save with periodic autosave keeps stakes honest. One backup rotation guards against corruption. Cloud sync is out of scope.
- Art: mix build-and-buy, Blender for the signatures (#38, #87-art). Hand-model in Blender the things that carry identity - the hive roster (Appendix A plates), the bee close-ups, the grandfather/mansion; use Asset Store packs for generic city props, foliage, and vehicles. This is the only way a solo dev survives the semi-realistic city scope.

Farming cut; a decor and garden shop in its place

- Vertical farming and crop growing are cut (#86, #87, #94, #95). Nothing is grown. It was a second full production chain - its own growth clock, its own harvest action, its own economy - stacked on a core loop (hive → honey → sell → reinvest) that is not playable yet. This is the first concrete answer to the cut-line question (#95): farming goes before the mansion/solarpunk ending or the shop/logistics/driving loop. Deferred, not forbidden - if the apiary loop turns out to feel thin, crops are a known place to add depth.
- What replaces it: buy, don’t grow. The player buys furniture and garden pieces from a shop catalogue and places them. This keeps the "make the place yours" feeling and the gold sink at a fraction of the cost - a catalogue of static placeable props reuses the hive-placement machinery (ghost preview, ground raycast, EconomyManager.TrySpend) instead of needing a second simulation. The tradeoff, stated honestly: no growing or tending gameplay, so decor is a one-time purchase decision rather than an ongoing activity.
- The functional/cosmetic rule. Nectar/pollen plants and water features are functional and affect the bee sim; everything built, and every non-melliferous ornamental, is purely cosmetic. Shop entries carry a "bee-friendly" tag showing the bloom window, so the split is learnable rather than arbitrary - and it doubles as the educational hook the document already wants (#92): not every pretty flower feeds bees.
- Functional: flower beds. Each is one placeable prop with a bloom window on the in-game calendar plus a forage contribution inside a radius. First-pass roster, all real Ukrainian bee plants (placeholder for the sim-balance pass): willow/crocus - very early spring, tiny yield, but it bridges the gap when colonies are weakest; fruit blossom - spring; acacia/black locust - late spring, high yield, short window; linden/lypa - early summer, high yield, and Sknyliv is full of them; phacelia - fast and long-blooming, the gap-filler; clover - steady mid-summer baseline; sunflower - late summer, big yield; goldenrod - late season, builds winter stores. This dovetails with the Lviv honey varietals idea in Appendix H.
- Functional: water. A bee waterer, stone basin or small pond reduces the summer heat penalty for hives in radius. Bees genuinely collect water to cool the hive and to dilute honey for brood food, so this is real-practice content rather than an invented mechanic. Every water feature counts as functional, including ornamental-looking ones like a birdbath - the alternative invites "which pond is the real one" confusion.
- Held back pending weather. Windbreak hedges (cut the wind penalty on foraging days) and shade trees (summer overheating) are real apiary-siting advice, but they only mean anything if per-day weather (QUESTIONS.md #7) lands. Not built until that is decided.
- Cosmetic, with no simulation effect at all. Indoor furniture for the mansion rebuild and the apiary support building; garden furniture - benches, tables, hammock, fire pit, fences, paths, gates, lamps; ornamental non-bee planting - hostas, ferns, grasses, topiary, potted foliage; statues, signage, seasonal decor.
- Placement: category-gated. Garden pieces and garden furniture go outdoors on owned land; indoor furniture goes inside the mansion and the apiary support building; the categories do not cross. Accepts a slightly less freeform builder in exchange for never having to answer "does this indoor prop survive rain" or weather-proof every mesh.
- What this leaves open. Recorded as QUESTIONS.md #39-#43: confirming the plant roster and whether bloom windows follow real Lviv timing (#24); the forage radius relative to a breed’s foraging range and whether overlapping beds stack, cap or diminish (too generous and decor beats hive siting); whether cosmetics pay off in any non-visual way; whether the shop is gated and whether decor can be sold back or moved; and whether water is a bonus or a requirement colonies suffer without. The flower-bed bonus also promotes QUESTIONS.md #8 (is foraging visually pathed, or an abstract range + density number?) to blocks-build, since a bed has to raise something concrete.
## Appendix H. Additional Ideas Not Yet in the Document

Net-new mechanics and flourishes that fit the Sknyliv setting and the systems already designed. Each is concrete and buildable on top of the existing colony sim/economy, with a one-line “why it fits.” These are proposals, not locked decisions - the gdd skill should be used to promote any the user actually settles on.

Setting-specific forage & product ideas

- Lviv honey varietals from real local forage. The central Vyhovsky/Sknyliv park and street trees give distinct nectar sources - linden (lypa), acacia (акація), buckwheat (гречка), and a mixed “wildflower” - each producing a named honey with its own colour, price, and demand curve. Why it fits: it turns the real map’s tree cover into an economic system for near-zero new code - forage density already varies per-zone (#79), so tag zones with a nectar type and the varietals fall out.
- Honey-varietal certification. Keep a hive on a single forage type long enough and its honey can be certified single-origin (e.g. “certified linden”), unlocking a premium price tier and eligibility for the best contracts. Why it fits: gives the quality/purity price lever (#15) a concrete goal and rewards deliberate hive placement over the park.
- Seasonal honey rotation goal. Because varietals bloom at different times (acacia late spring, linden early summer, buckwheat late summer), a completionist can chase a full seasonal set. Why it fits: uses the season system already designed (#80) to structure a soft collection goal without new systems.

Events & the wider world

- Farmers-market day at a real-mapped POI. On a recurring calendar day, a market stands up at a mapped location (near the АТБ retail cluster or the southern Гетьман corner); the player physically brings product for premium prices, meets contract customers, and buys supplies. Why it fits: makes the sell-and-restock loop a place you go rather than a menu, and gives the car’s cargo capacity (#88) a reason to exist.
- Medovyi Spas / honey-festival annual event. The real Ukrainian Honey Saviour feast (mid-August) becomes a once-a-year in-game festival - a big market day with a best-honey judging that pays out on your highest-quality certified varietal and boosts reputation. Why it fits: a culturally authentic tentpole event that rewards the whole quality chain and lands squarely on the real-place premise.
- Observation-hive tourism income. The urban sculptural tower and wall-mounted observation hives (Tier 1, Appendix A) placed in high-traffic spots generate a small passive tourism/education income when kept healthy and calm. Why it fits: gives those distinctive “city apiary” hive forms a mechanical reason to exist beyond looks, and ties into the pollination-score/education layer already noted in Appendix E.
- Disease quarantine radius affecting neighbours. An untreated foulbrood or heavy mite outbreak raises infection pressure on your own nearby hives and on the rival apiary; ignoring it can trigger a temporary placement quarantine on that zone. Why it fits: makes the named-disease system (#6) spatial and consequential, and gives the regulation layer (#23) a second, health-driven dimension.

People, teaching & the long arc

- Mentor-grandfather quest chain. A light, optional chain of the grandfather’s letters/tasks that each teach one real technique (splitting, requeening, treating varroa) and reward a small unlock. Why it fits: paces the mechanical unlocks the document already lists behind warm narrative beats (#63–#65), so learning the sim and hearing the story are the same act.
- Apprentice / helper you can hire later. Once established, hire a single helper who automates one chosen routine chore (routine inspections, carrying frames, feeding for winter) for a wage. Why it fits: answers the staff question (#18) with a scoped, single-hire version and gives a mid-game gold sink that buys time rather than power.
- Beekeeping-almanac book progression. Books the player buys/reads aren’t just unlocks - the almanac fills in as real, readable pages about each breed and disease as you encounter them, doubling as the diagnosis reference. Why it fits: makes the educational layer (#92) opt-in flavour that also carries genuine gameplay value (a diagnosis crib), satisfying both the ignore-it and the learn-from-it player.

Small flourishes

- Audible queenless hive. A hive that has lost its queen shifts to a distinct “roar” hum audible on approach before you open it. Why it fits: a real beekeeping cue that turns the audio direction (#31) into a free diagnostic tool and rewards attentive players.
- Winter-cluster visualisation. In winter, carefully opening a hive shows the bees clustered on the comb, shrinking visibly as stores run low - the readable face of the gradual winter drain (#82). Why it fits: makes the most tense season legible on-foot and gives feeding an obvious, satisfying visual payoff.
- Solarpunk mansion as a living scorecard. Each rebuilt fixture (green roof, solar array, wildflower-meadow yard, rain garden) also improves something small in the world - local forage, pollination score, or ambience. Why it fits: makes the endgame sink (#90–#91) feed back into the core loop instead of being a cosmetic dead-end, so the finale rewards the systems that got you there.
